About

I am a computational astrophysicist at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian, where I have been a NASA Hubble and ITC Postdoctoral Fellow since 2019. Since 2026 I am also a STEM Fellow at Anthropic. I received my PhD from the University of Chicago in 2019.

I work at the interface between the physics of the interstellar medium and the formation and evolution of galaxies. Together with my collaborators, I design, run, and analyze supercomputer simulations that span scales from individual star-forming regions to cosmological volumes.

Research

Modeling Early Turbulent Formation and Evolution of Galaxies

JWST has revealed a surprisingly large population of UV-bright galaxies in the early Universe, many of them already showing disk morphologies. We show that modeling star formation together with the turbulent state of the interstellar medium is what produces both the early variable star formation rates and the formation and survival of early disks.

How Unusual is the Milky Way’s Disk?

Gaia, H3, and APOGEE suggest the disk of our Galaxy formed within the first 2 billion years — far earlier than high-resolution cosmological simulations found. Using TNG50 we find that Milky-Way-mass galaxies do form later on average, but the scatter is large: about 10% form disks as early as the Milky Way did.

Why do galaxies form stars inefficiently?

We outline a framework connecting galaxy-scale star formation rates to the timescales of gas cycling on the scales of star-forming regions. It explains the global inefficiency of star formation, the near-linear correlation between star formation and molecular gas on kiloparsec scales, and the self-regulation of star formation in simulations.

Cosmic ray feedback

Cosmic rays carry a significant fraction of the pressure and energy budget of the interstellar medium. We show that the reduced propagation observed near star-forming regions qualitatively changes the structure of galaxies, suppressing the formation of dense gas clumps in gas-rich, unstable disks.
